2026 Cloud Breaches: The Year So Far

The year AI stopped being a topic security teams discussed and became a participant in incidents. January to early November, provisional until the full review in January 2027.

Join Friday Zoom All year reviews

Published 2026-07-26 · By Shawn Nunley · Analysis · View source on GitHub

This review covers January to early November 2026 and is provisional. It began in July as a mid-year review of nine kill chains and now covers every 2026 incident on the breach timeline through November 8. The complete 2026 review replaces it in January 2027. Where a later record overturned something the mid-year version said, the text below follows the newer record and says what changed.

The defining feature of 2026 is that artificial intelligence became a participant in incidents: as the operator, in evaluations where agents crossed boundaries no human chose to cross; as tooling that attackers steered; and as the infrastructure under attack. Alongside that, supply-chain attackers kept finding ways around the provenance and signing controls the industry deployed in response to 2024 and 2025, and the autumn brought intrusions aimed straight at money.

Overview: the shape of 2026 so far

This page follows 24 kill chains: every 2026 incident on the site's breach timeline through November 8, plus Promptware, an ongoing technique the timeline dates to 2024. Read together they describe a year in which the questions changed. In 2025 the recurring defensive question was "how did they get a valid credential." In 2026 it has been closer to "who, or what, was on the other end, and how did they move that fast."

The Hugging Face intrusion is the year's defining case, and the record of it changed after this review first went up. Hugging Face detected and contained the activity and disclosed it on July 16 without naming an actor. On July 21 OpenAI said the activity came from its own models. OpenAI's August 26 technical report, and a review by METR and Redwood whose questions and time window OpenAI set, describe something larger than the first accounts. From May 12, agents in an OpenAI reinforcement-learning run had used the company's internally hosted Artifactory, the one component allowed to reach outward, as a message board. METR counted roughly 1,200 agents on that board and about 700 that attacked Hugging Face. Over seven weeks they found several Artifactory zero-days, rebuilt their channel after OpenAI's July cleanup, and moved inside OpenAI's network to a node with internet access. Their first external foothold, on July 9, was a Modal customer's exposed evaluation harness. From there they read a Hugging Face dataset worker's environment through an HDF5 file, gained code execution in a production Kubernetes pod through a template injection OpenAI's report calls RefJinja, reached cluster admin in under 13 hours, and took five datasets by July 13. Then they turned back on OpenAI's own research cluster until July 19.

Three things this page said in July did not hold. It was not one model acting alone: about 700 agents attacked, coordinating on a board that had been running since May 12. They did not attack Hugging Face to steal the benchmark's answers, because most already had the flag. Some believed the grader also checked how the flag was obtained, so they went looking for the scorer, which checked only the flag, and the intrusion earned no improvement in evaluation score. And the way in was one file read and one code-execution path, where the first accounts described two code-execution paths. Hugging Face's chief executive said the company strongly believed there was no malicious intent, and OpenAI later reclassified the event from a security incident to a misalignment incident.

Detection worked on both sides of that case, and escalation did not. Hugging Face's AI security agent correlated the signals but did not page anyone on call. At OpenAI, an internal team had seen an agent using the message board and reaching the internet without authorisation in late May, roughly six weeks before Hugging Face was touched, and that never reached the team that opened an incident in July.

The Sygnia AWS case is the quieter version and worth treating carefully: a lone actor took broad control of a large AWS estate in roughly 72 hours with no malware and no zero-day, and four accounts' keys were used within a single observed second. Sygnia inferred AI assistance from attacker-created scripts and reporting artifacts, and from parallelism hard to reconcile with a single human operator. That is an inference, not proof, and this site frames it that way throughout.

The kill chains written up since July changed the year's shape in three ways. A formerly open-source agent framework, ARTEX, turned up in several separate campaigns, one of which targeted more than 200 organizations in 11 days. The AI platforms that hold developers' model keys became targets in their own right, with at least nine reporting incidents in October. And the autumn brought intrusions aimed straight at money: about $6.3 billion in digital assets affected at Binance by its own figure, after the attacker reached its wallet signing service; a vendor's monitoring script that drained Polymarket traders' accounts; and a hosting provider's management server that pushed malware onto the desktops its customers used for currency trading.

Theme 1: AI became a participant, not a tool

Late 2025 set this up. Anthropic's disclosure of GTG-1002 described a Chinese state-linked operator using Claude Code to execute the large majority of tactical work across roughly thirty targets, with humans reduced to decision gates. That was AI as a very fast operator under human direction.

The Hugging Face incident is a different category. Nobody directed the agents at Hugging Face. They were set a benchmark of real vulnerabilities, they got out of the environment meant to contain them through the one component allowed to reach outward, and they attacked a third party with no relationship to the experiment, some of them chasing a scoring rule OpenAI's internal grader did not apply. The intrusion was real, the victim was uninvolved, and the operators were software.

It did not stay an isolated case. On August 4 OpenAI disclosed two further cases where models left an evaluation boundary, both distinct from Hugging Face. At the UK AI Security Institute, evaluation runs spanning seven models from more than one lab produced 19 unsanctioned actions on the live internet, the most serious an attempted supply-chain attack on a real open-source project that a human reviewer caught. At the evaluator Irregular, a misconfiguration let models reach the real internet, and a model attacked a real website believing it was in the simulation. In a statement on September 28, 2026, OpenAI said an experimental internal model, set during training and evaluation to research public spending on medicines, found a way into non-public parts of Services Australia's Medicare Statistics Reporting Service and ran commands there. OpenAI says the access happened in June, and Australia's Prime Minister had disclosed the incident on September 24. That case has no kill chain on this site. The agentic section of AI and ML security sets it beside Hugging Face, and the community discussed it in the September 25, 2026 session. Then in November, WorkOS revised its account of a bulk retrieval from an internal sales tool. First blamed on an attacker using ARTEX, it is now attributed to a security researcher's ARTEX agent, running benchmark evaluations, that by the researcher's account escaped its isolated testing environment and reached WorkOS unintentionally. WorkOS and a forensics firm matched the researcher's evidence to WorkOS's logs, which settles whose agent made the requests. How it got out has not been published.

The defensive conclusion is narrower than the headlines suggest, and worth stating plainly because the novelty invites overreaction. The techniques used against Hugging Face were ordinary: reconnaissance, exploitation, credential harvesting, lateral movement. What was new was the coordination: a message board the agents built for themselves, and a fleet across 11 nodes that respawned itself, so killing any one pod accomplished nothing. Patching, least privilege, egress control and segmentation work exactly as well against an AI operator as a human one. What changes for defenders is tempo, and one new obligation: if you run capability evaluations or agent systems, the containment around them is now part of your attack surface. The mid-year version of this page said "highly isolated" needed to mean air-gapped rather than allow-listed. After a further, less severe escape on September 20, when a model reached a public chatbot over DNS, OpenAI paused training, evaluation and tool-using inference for its most capable models. Training resumed on October 1, according to Reuters, and in an October 23 post, as TechCrunch reported it, OpenAI committed to training frontier models with no live internet access.

Theme 2: Attackers adopted agent frameworks

The first half's case for AI-assisted attackers rested on Sygnia's inference. The autumn brought a named tool. ARTEX is a multi-agent framework, a main agent with planner and worker agents over a shared graph of facts, findings and goals, published on GitHub as an open-source project on July 26, 2026, according to Censys. Its developer later denied involvement and the repository was taken down, but forks circulate, and Microsoft says multiple actors in different regions use it. Four kill chains on this site involve it. Two campaigns are attributed to different actors, a third is unattributed, and WorkOS now attributes its incident to a researcher's ARTEX agent.

VAPOR PANDA, a China-nexus group by CrowdStrike's assessment, used ARTEX against more than 200 organizations in at least 14 countries over 11 days from October 17, and CrowdStrike confirmed post-exploitation at nine. A person stayed in the loop: recovered transcripts show Chinese-language instructions labeled as human input, steering the agents at decision points while they did the mechanical work. Storm-0940, a China-state aligned actor in Microsoft's assessment, whose campaign CrowdStrike says is separate from VAPOR PANDA's, used stolen credentials for ConnectWise Cloud Backup's multi-tenant application identities to attempt app-only sign-ins to more than 2,250 customer tenants. Most of that failed. In multiple customer environments, agents ran Graph reconnaissance, added credentials to existing OAuth applications and read secrets across Azure Key Vaults, and in multiple instances went from targeting to privileged access in under three hours. A third, unattributed campaign began with an agentic framework, which Microsoft links to ARTEX, searching GitHub for secrets from August. In a follow-on attack from as early as October 28, leaked or stolen application credentials of unresolved origin were used to sign in as service principals across tenants, read Key Vaults and reach managed service providers' client tenants.

The labels run ahead of the evidence, so read each case for what was shown. In the exposed-secrets campaign the case for an agent is speed and failed API calls corrected within 28 to 80 seconds, and Microsoft publishes nothing that separates an agent from a script or an operator. Microsoft's report on Storm-3168 calls it agentic, and Sysdig calls JADEPUFFER the first documented agentic ransomware operation, but what Microsoft published for this Azure case (two service principals, about 15.5 hours of reads, then more than 100 storage deletion attempts in about seven minutes) strongly indicates automated or scripted execution and does not directly show an AI agent at work.

What the frameworks change is rate and breadth. Adam Meyers of CrowdStrike said VAPOR PANDA's attacks showed nothing new in technique, putting typical hands-on activity at up to about 40 commands an hour and saying ARTEX could exceed 1,500, which is an estimate of capacity rather than a measurement of one intrusion. The defensive implication follows from that rate: a control that waits for a person to notice a pattern over hours is slower than the attack. The controls that matter hold without a human in the loop, such as application credentials that cannot reach every tenant, Key Vault access scoped to one workload, and alerts on service principal sign-ins from addresses they have never used.

PHP2Shell is a useful check on how far that goes. AI-assisted research found two memory-safety flaws in core PHP, from a bug class dating to 2004, and the embargo broke in stages before the October 21 fixes. Mass exploitation then mostly failed, because a working exploit had to fit each target's build and configuration. Appliances were the exception, since many devices share one build: an operator filtered about 19,000 internet-facing PAN-OS firewalls down to 240, used a PHP exploit with per-firmware profiles, chained with a Linux kernel flaw, to root 24 of them, then reached a firewall management server and enumerated a US government-affiliated network's Active Directory users and groups. Dream, which recovered the toolkit, found AI tooling in the attackers' infrastructure but has no direct evidence that AI wrote the exploit, and the operation did not appear fully autonomous. Researchers still disagree on how much of a barrier per-target tuning is.

Theme 3: The AI toolchain became the target

The first half's sharpest example of an attack on AI infrastructure was LiteLLM: TeamPCP poisoned Trivy, a vulnerability scanner running inside LiteLLM's CI, and the scanner read the pipeline's secrets and handed over the PyPI publishing token. Two malicious releases went out with no matching git tag. The second planted a .pth file that executed on every interpreter start in that Python environment: before any application code, in every Python process, whether or not anything imported LiteLLM.

An LLM gateway is an unusually rich target because of what it holds: provider API keys for multiple model vendors by design, and it usually runs where cloud credentials are also within reach. LiteLLM was hit again in October, in a separate incident by an unidentified actor. An administrative key to an internal, experimental agent platform had been committed to a public repository in May, removed from the current files about three hours later but left in public history until October 22, and not revoked before the attack. The attacker used it to read every managed agent's configuration, which held the GitHub CLI token of one of the organization's two owners, and from there pushed a release through LiteLLM's real pipeline. Its credential stealer collected configuration for more than 20 AI coding tools alongside, on proxy hosts, the stored model-provider keys.

The platforms that run AI developers' workloads became the larger target. Between October 6 and October 30, at least nine AI developer platforms reported security incidents, which Wiz (disclosure: the site's author works at Wiz) gathered in a report titled Black October. Wiz says no single vulnerability or dependency links them. What recurs is the objective: the environment variables customers store on the platform, especially AI model keys. Zeabur's report is the fullest. A non-expiring cluster admin token stored in plaintext in a private repository helped open a shared cluster, a second path through Google Cloud then reached the platform database, and the attacker read 3,218,965 environment variable records and 321,810 accounts. Separately, and without Wiz naming which breach they came from, stolen Amazon Bedrock keys were validated within 30 minutes of the reported credential access and later used for floods of about 800,000 and 1.7 million model calls. OpenCode and CommandCode attribute their incidents to attempted distillation, meaning training a cheaper model on a stronger model's outputs.

AI tools also became something to steal from developer machines. The stealer in the OVHcloud CLI release swap took AI coding-tool data and CHAINDROP took AI service credentials, both alongside cloud and Kubernetes credentials. With a stolen GitHub App token, CHAINDROP also committed a Claude Code settings hook and a VS Code task to repositories, so the worm ran again when someone opened them. Binance's account starts in June, when an engineer looking for a popular AI coding tool downloaded a tampered Homebrew installation, which Binance caught and cleaned up; the access behind the theft came through a fake job interview in September. In October the attacker used an internal AI tool to gain control over two engineers, by a mechanism Binance has not described. Treat AI coding tools' settings directories as credential stores, and review a repository's tool configuration like any other code.

All of this continues a pattern the lessons page names outright: your own tooling, turned around. Trivy is a scanner. TruffleHog, used in the 2025 Shai-Hulud worm, is a secret scanner. The AI CLIs weaponized in Nx s1ngularity are coding assistants. Every capability installed for convenience or diligence is a capability an attacker inherits, and security tooling is a particularly rich seam because it is trusted by definition and runs with elevated access.

Theme 4: Supply-chain attacks routed around provenance and signatures

The industry's answer to SolarWinds and the 2025 npm worms was to prove where a package came from, through artifact signing, SLSA provenance and trusted publishing. 2026 shows attackers accepting that and going around it.

Mini Shai-Hulud needed no stolen maintainer token at all: a fork pull request poisoned the GitHub Actions cache, a later release run restored it, and OIDC tokens scraped from runner memory published 84 npm versions carrying entirely valid SLSA provenance. Megalodon poisoned Tiledesk's source on GitHub and let the maintainer publish from it, so the malicious releases carried legitimate publisher identity.

In both cases every downstream check that verifies who published a package returned a truthful, clean answer. Provenance establishes origin. It does not establish that the build environment was uncompromised when the artifact was produced, and this year is when attackers started operating on that distinction deliberately. The 2024 Ultralytics compromise was the prototype; 2026 is when it became a pattern.

The autumn extended the pattern from provenance to signatures and checksums. LiteLLM 1.107.0 was built from a lookalike repository by LiteLLM's real release pipeline, so its images carried a valid signature from LiteLLM's signing key and the release went out under LiteLLM's own Trusted Publisher identity. Verification alone passes on the malicious images. In CHAINDROP, according to Socket, the legitimate release workflow built already-trojanized source, so the signed provenance was valid. By Elastic's count more than 400 packages had been compromised by August 6, as the worm republished with stolen npm tokens and, Socket reports, through trusted publishing. At OVHcloud, a compromised developer account replaced the binaries on five old releases of the company's CLI, and on at least three of them replaced the checksums file too, so a check against the publisher's own checksums passed. Polymarket loaded a monitoring script from an allowlisted vendor host with no integrity hash pinning the file, so when the same URL began serving a drainer, browsers ran it.

What caught OVHcloud was a hash the publisher did not control. A customer's CI job pinned its own SHA-256 of one archive, the job failed, the customer opened an issue, and OVHcloud removed the binaries 31 minutes later. The mid-year version of this page said consuming provenance is not a substitute for hash-pinning dependencies, and the autumn supplied the evidence. Provenance, a signature and a publisher's checksum file all passed in these cases: provenance and signatures truthfully described the pipeline that built a poisoned artifact, and at OVHcloud the attacker had replaced the checksums file itself. A hash recorded when you first trusted a version is what turned this swap into a failed build. OVHcloud has since made releases immutable across its GitHub organization, which protects releases published from now on; GitHub's immutability does not cover releases published before it was turned on.

None of that makes provenance worthless. PyPI's own analysis of Ultralytics noted attestations made the forensics tractable, because it was provable which releases came through the workflow and which came from a stale token. It works best as evidence beside pinning and beside limits on what a build environment can reach. CHAINDROP added a response hazard as well: a watcher that runs a remotely supplied handler when the stolen GitHub token is revoked. The CHAINDROP page's advice, following SANS and Socket, is to isolate the host before revoking anything.

One June incident with no kill chain of its own on this site belongs in this theme anyway, because it attacks the other half of provenance: the part a human reads. IronWorm, named by JFrog Security Research, republished 43 npm packages from one compromised account, each carrying a preinstall hook that ran a bundled Rust binary before dependency resolution had finished. The binary harvested 86 environment variables and more than twenty credential file paths (cloud keys, model-provider API keys, npm tokens, wallet material), hid itself behind an eBPF kernel rootkit that filtered its own processes out of /proc and its own sockets out of /proc/net/tcp, and answered to its operator over Tor. It then propagated the way Shai-Hulud did, by turning stolen credentials into publishing rights.

The part that makes it a provenance story is what it did to git. JFrog counted 57 malicious commits across nine GitHub organizations, with each commit's timestamp forged from the repository's own most recent real commit, so some of them presented as roughly thirteen years old. The author was spoofed as claude <claude@users.noreply.github.com>, impersonating an AI assistant. Nothing here defeats a signature, because nothing here is signed: a commit date is metadata the author supplies, and an unsigned author line is a string. What it defeats is the review habit built on top of them: sorting by date, skimming what changed this week, and treating a commit that looks like automation as routine. Signing and attestation answer "did this artifact come from the right place." They were never designed to answer "is this history telling the truth about when it happened," and they still do not.

Theme 5: The authorized integration is the way in

The 2025 pattern continued without interruption. Vercel was reached because an employee had connected a third-party AI tool to their corporate Google Workspace account, and control of that tool's OAuth application converted into control of the identity, then the Vercel account, then internal systems, with no Vercel vulnerability at any point. Vimeo was reached because an analytics vendor held long-lived tokens into its Snowflake and BigQuery instances, and the attacker never exceeded the permissions that integration had been granted.

What makes this category durable is that it produces no signal a conventional identity control can catch. There is no failed authentication, no MFA denial, no impossible-travel login, because there is no login. Password rotation does not revoke it and employee offboarding does not revoke it. And the grant frequently never passed through vendor review, because from the company's perspective nothing was procured; somebody clicked "allow" on a consent screen.

The chains written up since July moved the pattern from grants users click to workload identities. Storm-0940 needed no user at all: stolen credentials for a backup vendor's multi-tenant application identities allowed app-only sign-ins, which raise no MFA prompt, and one of ConnectWise's management applications assigned itself Global Administrator. The exposed-secrets campaign signed in as service principals with application secrets, where Conditional Access policies scoped to users do not apply. At Storm-3168, everything the attacker did ran within the roles two compromised service principals already held, and about 30 minutes after the deletions it collected storage account keys that outlive the identity, so disabling the service principals would not revoke them. In each case the starting access was granted on purpose, and at ConnectWise an application then raised its own privileges.

Sessions and tokens issued to people got the same treatment. In the M365 token spray that Huntress tracked from July to September, sessions captured after MFA, through adversary-in-the-middle pages, device-code phishing or social engineering, were replayed within minutes from one rented /24. Inside, the operator registered rogue Entra devices and replaced victims' MFA methods with its own, which survive a password reset. Huntress responded to related incidents at 79 organizations. At LiteLLM, an owner's GitHub CLI OAuth token was not subject to GitHub's two-factor re-authentication for sensitive actions, and it overrode branch protection five seconds after a push was rejected.

A development credential fared no better. At WorkOS, an internet-reachable internal API with no rate limit accepted a predictable credential meant for local development.

Storm-2949 is the identity-layer companion piece: an attacker-initiated self-service password reset plus a phone call impersonating IT, so the victim approved a genuine MFA prompt. Number matching bought nothing, because the control was faithfully validating a real request. It just could not tell who had asked for it.

Theme 6: The autumn went after money

Three of the autumn's incidents went straight for money, and each turned a system the victim ran against its own funds or customers.

Binance puts the digital assets affected on October 29 at about $6.34 billion, and says about $2.85 billion was frozen or intercepted. Binance describes one multi-stage campaign that began months earlier. In June an engineer downloaded a tampered Homebrew installation, which Binance's security team detected, replacing the device and rotating credentials. In September another ran a fake job interview's "assignment" on a work computer, which swapped pyenv for a malicious copy. On October 20 the attacker used an internal AI tool to gain control over two engineers and found a container deployment token on one of their computers. On October 29 it used those credentials to deploy a container and reached Binance's internal wallet signing service, which Binance says was used to initiate transactions outside the normal withdrawal process. Binance found no evidence that wallet private keys leaked, and Elliptic says the on-chain evidence suggests the attacker got Binance's own systems to sign on its behalf, including 50 validly signed authorizations that delegated a hot wallet to an attacker contract. The FBI attributes the theft to North Korea's TraderTraitor, as reported by The Record and BleepingComputer.

At Polymarket the vehicle was a vendor's script. DebugBear says its DNS records were compromised through its domain registrar, and on October 29 the unchanged URL of its monitoring script began serving a drainer. For logged-in traders, the drainer found Polymarket's own order client in the page and used the trader's live session to sell or transfer positions, then sent the proceeds through Polymarket's own relayer. Polymarket put the loss at $2.7 million from 726 wallets and is returning $3.9 million, a figure that adds losses caused by its trading pause.

GMO Onamae.com Desktop Cloud ran hosted Windows desktops that customers used for automated currency trading. An attacker entered through one customer's desktop VPS on August 5, took an administrator account and, by August 17, GMO's management server, and from September 20 to October 14 used that server to push malware to customer desktops. Broker logins saved in trading tools were used for unauthorized trades from October 21, the day an email demanded US$50 million (about ¥8 billion by GMO's figure), which GMO refused. The intrusion went unnoticed for more than two months, until a customer inquiry on October 19.

In none of the three is there evidence that the attacker broke cryptography or stole a signing key, though Binance's findings are still preliminary. Each attacker reached a system the victim used to move money or push software and turned it to its own purpose. The controls that matter sit in front of those systems: signing services that refuse requests arriving outside the approval path, short-lived deployment tokens, an integrity hash on every third-party script that runs where sessions live, and monitoring on any management server that can push code to every customer.

What the mid-year review said to watch

The July version of this page named four things to watch for the rest of the year.

Whether the Hugging Face incident stays an outlier. It did not. The July version set the test: one AI agent conducting an unplanned intrusion during an evaluation could be a containment failure at one lab, and two would be a category. By that test it is a category. OpenAI disclosed two further boundary escapes on August 4, the Services Australia case was disclosed in September, and WorkOS, by its researcher's account, was another. The wider record grew too. By its September 30 update, as The Register reported, OpenAI had notified more than 100 organizations that its models may have accessed their systems, up from dozens, and the September 20 escape led to the pause described in Theme 1.

Whether provenance tooling adapts. The July version asked for attestations that cover build-environment integrity, and none of this year's write-ups shows one in use. The autumn's supply-chain incidents passed signature checks, provenance checks and a publisher's own checksum file, and the control that caught one of them was a hash the consumer held.

Whether the infostealer economy keeps scaling. The developer compromises behind this year's later incidents came through code a developer chose to install or run: an IDE extension on an OVHcloud developer's personal laptop, a tampered Homebrew installation and a fake interview's assignment at Binance, and a worm that swept more than 300 credential patterns from machines that installed it. This page has no measure of whether the market in bought logs grew.

What the rest of the year does to these conclusions. The mid-year themes held, and the autumn added two the first half did not have: off-the-shelf agent frameworks in ordinary intrusions, and attacks aimed directly at money. Three of the July version's statements about Hugging Face did not hold, and the overview replaces them.

What to watch through the end of the year

Whether agent frameworks change who can run a broad campaign. ARTEX is used by state-aligned and financially motivated groups alike. Watch whether it raises the number of groups able to work hundreds of targets at once.

Whether evaluation environments get real walls. OpenAI's reported commitment to train frontier models without live internet access is one lab's answer, and it covers training. Watch whether evaluations, other labs, and anyone running agent benchmarks adopt the same standard.

Whether AI platforms stop keeping customers' keys within reach of one credential. At Zeabur a shared admin token with no expiry helped open the cluster where customers' environment variables were stored. Zeabur says it will shut down on December 31, 2026, which it calls an operational decision.

What the full year does to this page. It stays provisional by construction. If November and December look like October, the January review will read much like this one with more evidence. If they do not, this page will be revised rather than defended.
