What Breaking Into Cloud Security Really Takes

Not a career guide. A report on what the people already doing the job have said about getting into it, pulled out of 20 Friday sessions of Cloud Security Office Hours. Certifications, home labs, referrals, rejection counts, burnout, and the arguments the room has never settled.

Join the Friday call Where the room disagrees

Last updated 2026-07-24 · By Shawn Nunley · Synthesized from community sessions · View source on GitHub

The 30-second version: the room treats certifications as a filter key, not proof of competence, and has never agreed on how much they matter. Nearly every hiring story told on the call ran through a person, not a portal. The work that convinced people was reproducible and public. The market is described as genuinely hard, by members who were unemployed or burned out when they said it. Nobody in the archive claims there is a clean path.

Where this comes from

Cloud Security Office Hours is a live Zoom that meets every Friday. Most weeks there is no agenda, no recording unless a guest is presenting, and no vendor pitches. Careers come up anyway: the room holds hiring managers, people three months into a first security job, people who have been applying for a year, and people who are quietly burned out.

This page pulls that thread out of 20 session recaps, with a link behind every claim. The prescriptive version already exists here: a learning path, a certification guide, a resume guide. This is the hallway conversation instead.

Two cautions. This is a room, not a survey: none of it is data, and the people in it contradict each other. And individual members are not named, because career struggles get shared on that call in a semi-private space. Guests who came to present publicly are the exception.

The certification argument, still unsettled

The topic the community returns to most and resolves least.

In the October 4, 2024 session the word the room landed on was situational: certifications open doors but are not always necessary. One member described studying for a vendor identity exam, finding the content irrelevant to the actual work, and calling it a money grab. The same conversation still recommended an AWS cloud practitioner credential and a vendor-neutral cloud security knowledge certificate as entry points.

The December 13, 2024 session split inside a single hour. One member was openly skeptical they are needed at all; another said he had never needed one to get hired. Others argued for two uses: a syllabus for self-directed study, and getting past the barrier that stops you talking to an employer. The framing that stuck was that a certification confirms reasonable intelligence rather than measuring competence, and a separate remark held that one long-standing audit certification was losing its shine because it could be earned without practical experience. One member's advice was to build a home lab before paying for any exam.

The screening-tool reading keeps surviving. In October 2025 the room held that certifications help at the first screen but are not always required at large cloud providers, where skills can be built on the job. In September 2025 a member who had applied extensively without an offer said an AWS security certification and a bachelor's degree were the two things getting him past HR screens. A filter key, not a competence signal.

The strongest pro-certification case came from a guest: in the October 10, 2025 fireside chat, Andrea Pullman of the nonprofit Cyberjutsu described failing the CISSP before eventually passing it.

The experience paradox

The archive holds three answers, none of them fast.

Reframe work you already did. In the October 2024 session, a member worried that years of substantial security work would not count without a formal security title. The room said it can count, and that an endorsement from someone who knows the work is the practical mechanism. Another attendee had already validated her experience the same way.

Take the long route on purpose. The August 29, 2025 session was a member presenting his own transition: laid off from a human resources role, into a data center technician job at a cloud provider, through a cloud DevOps bootcamp, then certifications paired with practical projects and a deliberately built public presence. He was direct about the volume of rejections, and described the necessary mindset change as going from sprinter to marathon runner.

Find a program built for the gap. In the July 11, 2025 session, a member described their employer's cyber defense practitioner rotation: two intakes a year, two weeks of shadowing, a living requirements document, a new team every six months. It exists because new hires arrive with theory and no practice.

The paradox runs the other way too. In the March 1, 2024 session, a member began his first full-time security role as the only security specialist at a 200-person company, and came to the call for mentorship because there was nobody internally to learn from.

Labs, repos, and proof that travels

When the room tells someone what to build, the answers are concrete and public.

The December 2024 session produced detail rather than encouragement: Raspberry Pi clusters for Kubernetes with a caveat about ARM, one member's switch from servers to Intel Mac Minis, and a weekly cloud security lab series as structured practice. Asked in the October 2025 session what to build to learn Kubernetes, the room said EKS blueprints and a real CI pipeline, not tutorials. A newcomer in the November 21, 2025 session described his path as Linux, Python, and hands-on labs, and nobody argued.

Public work counts as much as private practice. The December 12, 2025 session was a community-run GitHub workshop: fork, change, open a pull request on a live project, built so people who do not write code could contribute in Markdown. The February 27, 2026 session added that a pull request does not have to be code, and that knowing how to open one is a skill in itself. In the November 22, 2024 session, members were invited to contribute tutorials and demos to this site, and several volunteered on the spot.

Presenting the work is the last mile. In the January 16, 2026 session, a member brought an internal security awareness project for critique and left with the advice to establish business value metrics before chasing complete coverage, and to design around the audience rather than the technical taxonomy.

Referrals, and what a referral costs

The closest thing to consensus in the archive, and uncomfortable if you were hoping the application portal works.

In December 2024, one member described an offer that arrived through a connection and another said he had stopped applying because roles now came through his network; the word the room used was champion, or sponsor. The August 23, 2024 session reached the same place with a harder edge: find an internal sponsor to secure the interview, and understand that remote hiring means you now compete globally. In the June 6, 2025 session, guest speaker Matt Chiodi said he has generally found senior roles, including COO and CISO positions, through networking rather than posted listings.

The room is equally clear that a referral is a favor with a price. The November 21, 2025 session set the boundaries: do not refer someone you do not know well, do your own due diligence, and keep straight that a referral is not a reference. One member described the aftermath of referring a candidate who turned out to be optimizing for salary over the technical work.

Two tactics. From October 2025: at a conference, carry cards with a LinkedIn link or QR code rather than resumes, and go talk to the speakers. From the April 5, 2024 session, on treating a job hunt as a sales pipeline: qualify the targets worth your effort, go deeper than other candidates by reading what the company's own customers say about its product, and accept that interviews are subjective enough that being memorable matters as much as being right.

What the hiring managers look for

Several regulars hire. What they describe wanting is not recall.

The May 30, 2025 session produced the most concrete example in the archive. In a hiring exercise, candidates were asked to build an EKS cluster; the ones who stood out reached for Terraform, so the result was reproducible and version controlled rather than clicked together once. A manager on the same call pointed to an intern who had built a custom solution to a routine task and named that instinct, curiosity paired with problem-solving, as an important quality when hiring. In the July 2025 session the room heard that one fast-growing vendor screens hard on cultural fit rather than technical expertise alone.

How you ask questions is itself evaluated. That same session circulated guidance on asking well: give context, show your work, phrase it so it is easy to answer. February 2026 came back to it through the XY problem. Not knowing is fine; asking badly is the avoidable part.

On resumes, the February 14, 2025 session was a live review: quantify claims and be ready to tell the story behind the number, tailor per application, put certifications toward the top, keep fonts consistent, be open about a layoff rather than hiding the gap, and include non-technology experience when it shows transferable skill.

Communication came up often enough to be a theme. May 2025 concluded it is crucial for advancement even in deeply technical roles, and suggested recording yourself and practicing an elevator pitch. September 2025 added the framing that works on executives: business outcomes and stories, not fear.

The market, as members describe it

The community does not sand this down, so this page will not either.

The October 3, 2025 session named the emotional toll a hard market takes on job seekers, coined job hugging for staying put out of fear rather than fit, and noted that AI-generated resumes are now common enough that applying by volume has lost its edge. August 2024 was blunter: a member described the pressure to provide free labor inside interview processes, and the same conversation noted that remote work had turned hiring into global competition. In September 2025, a member said directly that he had been unable to secure employment despite extensive applications, and the August 2025 transition story ran through many rejections before it ended well.

The counterweight came from that same October call: a member reported that several large employers had shifted hiring toward early-career candidates and were more open to recent graduates, and advised applying confidently rather than being deterred by the interview loops. Both readings were spoken in the same room on the same day. That is the actual state of the evidence.

What career guides leave out

Burnout is a normal reason people move. In the August 2024 session, a long-tenured member described leaving a large employer after burnout and a loss of faith in management. Another member on that call was burning out in a cloud security engineering role at a managed security provider; the advice he got was to interview strategically and put mental health first. In September 2025, a member described leaving a role after absorbing too much responsibility, and the room's read was that being the person who does all the security work builds something that collapses the day you leave.

A criminal record is a real question with no clean answer. The July 18, 2025 session took the question of whether a cybersecurity student with a record could be hired. The answer was neither yes nor no: pursue expungement where possible, disclose upfront, expect to need exceptional skill and a strong network, and expect many employers to keep applying rigid compliance criteria anyway. Entrepreneurship was raised as a legitimate alternative rather than a consolation prize.

Neurodiversity is treated as an asset with conditions. The November 2025 session included a suggestion that a substantial share of the field is neurodivergent, and several members shared their own experience. Pattern recognition and hyperfocus were framed as advantages in technical work, with the task being to find an environment that accommodates you rather than masking.

Some rules nobody writes down. February 2025 and October 2025 both spent real time on boundaries at work and conference events: set personal limits with alcohol, be clear about your intentions when networking, do not leave a drink unattended. And the October 2025 fireside chat covered the persistent gender gap and the case for organizations offering a safe space and peer mentorship to underrepresented groups. The counterpart is the culture the call tries to hold: April 2025 and February 2026 both framed the group as a place to ask what an unfamiliar term means without judgment.

Where the room disagrees

Unresolved. Presenting them as settled would be the dishonest version of this page.

Whether a degree is necessary

The clearest live split in the archive. In the August 29, 2025 session, a member asked whether to pursue a four-year degree or focus on open source contribution instead, and the advice came back genuinely mixed. Some argued a degree is close to essential now, particularly for entry-level roles filtered by HR. Others argued practical experience and network matter more. One member made a third case: the real value of those years is the community you build in them. Another spoke well of finishing both a bachelor's and a master's for the discipline and depth. Nobody conceded.

Whether certifications are worth it

December 2024 held skeptics and advocates in the same hour, and October 2024 had a member calling a specific exam a money grab while others recommended entry-level cloud credentials in the same conversation. The only shared ground is that a certification alone will not carry you.

Whether Kubernetes is a reasonable place to start

In the April 25, 2025 session, a member warned that Kubernetes takes real expertise to operate securely and cautioned people new to technology against reaching for it, adding that container boundaries are not security boundaries. Months later, the October 2025 session handed a newcomer EKS blueprints and a CI pipeline as a home-lab starting point. Both are defensible. They are not the same advice.

Whether the market is closing or opening

Contradicted inside one session: October 3, 2025 holds both the hardest description of the market in the archive and the most optimistic read on entry-level hiring at large employers.

Whether mentorship should be structured

In the June 2025 session, guest speaker Matt Chiodi argued that mentorship which works tends to happen organically, and that structured corporate programs can feel artificial and lack real commitment on either side. The room's counter-proposal was to scope such programs to short-term specific goals rather than open-ended career guidance, and one attendee suggested volunteering together as a low-cost compatibility test. Yet the October 2025 session described a deliberately structured peer mentorship program running to good effect. Structure is not the enemy; artificial structure is.

Where next

All of this was said out loud by people willing to be honest in front of strangers. That happens on Fridays, and it is free.
